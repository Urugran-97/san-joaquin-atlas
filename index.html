    Agriculture

    Almonds passed milk as the county's top crop in 2025

    The Agricultural Commissioner's 92nd annual crop report puts 2025 gross production at more than $3.35 billion, up 6.39% from $3,146,586,502 in 2024.

    Scenario lab

    Change the county and see what follows

    Illustrative scenarios, not predictions. Do not use these results for land-use, financial, agricultural or engineering decisions. See the Terms of Use.

    Set the population, land-use and habitat levers on the left. The model runs the county forward to 2050 eight hundred times with random year-to-year luck, then shows the middle path and the range that holds 80% of the runs. Everything it assumes is listed at the bottom.

    About “psychohistory”. Hari Seldon's science is from Isaac Asimov's Foundation novels and does not exist. What this lab borrows are its ideas: forecast the aggregate and not individuals, state the odds instead of one number, and watch for threshold points where the options narrow. The odds come from the county's own past swings, so they are a record of how uncertain the past was, not a promise about the future.

    Median result and what changed

    On the Residents chart, the dotted amber line is the San Joaquin Council of Governments' draft county forecast (January 2025 draft, REMI model): 804,252 in 2025 rising to 956,344 in 2050. It starts about 20,000 below the Census 2025 estimate and is a draft, so read it as a second opinion on the slope. Each cell is the median of the runs, with the 10th to 90th percentile range beneath. “Change” compares the 2050 median with the observed-trend preset. See it on the 3D map.

    Threshold points

    Edit a threshold to move it. “Median path” is the year the middle line crosses it. The percentages are the share of the 800 runs that have crossed it by that year.

    Every assumption in the model

    Start. Population 823,815 (Census, July 2025). Land starts from the 2018 state farmland survey: 615,785 acres in the four farmland classes, 126,902 acres of grazing land, 97,541 urban. I moved 2018 forward to 2025 by the county's growth since then (66,536 people, using the 2018 density of 7.76 people per urban acre and a 50/50 split of the land taken from farmland and grazing). That bridge is an estimate, not a survey.

    Population. Each year grows by your rate plus luck. The luck has two parts taken from 2010–2025 Census estimates (the 2020 rebase excluded): a per-run drift of ±0.3 points and a yearly wobble of ±0.32 points.

    Land. New residents need new urban land at your density. Only growth uses land; if the county shrinks, nothing is converted back. That land comes from farmland and grazing land in the share you set.

    Farm value. 2025 gross value ($3,356M, nominal) times the farmland remaining times your yearly growth, plus a swing that fades. The swing's size (about 11.6%) and its 0.61 year-to-year carry-over come from the 2005–2025 crop-report totals. Prices, weather, water supply and policy are not modelled, so the range reflects only how much the totals moved in the past.

    Habitat proxy. Grazing land plus the acres you conserve each year plus any one-time change in 2030. The default of 950 acres a year is the average of the 2024 (1,416 acres) and 2025 (491 acres) San Joaquin County Multi-Species Habitat plan reports. It stands in for species availability. No species counts are loaded, so the model cannot say which species gain or lose.

    City columns. City figures for 2025 are 2020 Census counts plus the county's gain since then, split by each place's share of 2010–2020 growth. Later growth is split by the pattern you choose.

    Left out. Water supply, flooding, jobs, housing prices, migration policy, climate, and any reaction by people to the forecast itself.

    Elevation and terrain Estimated or interpolated

    Source agency
    NASA Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (SRTM GL1, 1 arc-second), distributed by OpenTopography
    Date range
    Radar survey flown in February 2000
    Retrieved
    November 11, 2026
    License and terms
    OpenTopography lists the license as “not provided.” NASA SRTM data is generally free to use with citation. Needs review. Cite: NASA SRTM (2013), distributed by OpenTopography, https://doi.org/10.5069/G9445JDF.
    How it was obtained
    Downloaded as a GeoTIFF for the county's bounding box, cut to the county outline, then averaged in 9 × 9 blocks (about 250 m cells) and stored as whole metres. Heights are above sea level (EGM96 geoid).
    Known limitations
    Heights are of the surface as the radar saw it, including trees and buildings, and carry vertical errors of several metres. The Delta and valley floor vary by less than that, so contours under 10 m there are not reliable. A few isolated spikes remain over water. The county outline used to cut the data is coarse. Height exaggeration on the map is visual only.
